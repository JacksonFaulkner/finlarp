# 1. What happens: light stays trapped in a glass core for kilometres

Light from the laser enters the CORE, a glass channel about 8 um across. Around it the CLADDING, 125 um of glass with a slightly lower refractive index, acts as a mirror: light hitting the boundary at a shallow angle reflects back in (TOTAL INTERNAL REFLECTION). A plastic COATING, 242 um across, protects the glass. In SINGLE-MODE fiber the core is so narrow light travels as one beam straight down it; in MULTIMODE fiber a wider core lets many paths bounce along.

# 2. The parts

CORE                       glass about 8.2 um across (single-mode), 50 um (multimode); slightly higher index
CLADDING                   glass 125 um across; lower index keeps light in the core
COATING                    acrylate, 242 um (or 200 um); protects the glass from scratches and bends
TOTAL INTERNAL REFLECTION  light meeting a lower-index boundary at a shallow angle reflects completely
SINGLE-MODE                one path; 9.2 um mode field at 1310 nm; buildings and beyond
MULTIMODE                  many paths; cheap VCSEL light; up to about 100 m
ATTENUATION                loss along the fiber: at most 0.32 dB/km at 1310 nm, 0.18 dB/km at 1550 nm
LATENCY                    light in glass covers about 1 km every 5 microseconds

# 3. Light down the fiber, in order

1. a lens focuses the laser's light onto the core's end
2. light that enters within the acceptance angle stays in the core
3. it reflects (multimode) or guides (single-mode) along the core
4. a little is scattered and absorbed each kilometre
5. it leaves the far end into the receiver's lens

# 4. Why the light stays in

The core's index is only about a third of a percent above the cladding's, but that's enough: light travelling nearly along the fiber meets the boundary at a grazing angle and reflects completely. This is TOTAL INTERNAL REFLECTION. In single-mode fiber the core is only a few wavelengths across, so the light travels as one guided mode, partly in the cladding (the mode field is 9.2 um wide at 1310 nm).

# 5. Single-mode vs multimode

MULTIMODE fiber's 50 um core accepts light easily from cheap 850 nm VCSELs, but its many paths have different lengths, so pulses smear after about 100 m. SINGLE-MODE fiber's narrow core needs precise alignment and InP lasers, but pulses stay sharp over kilometres. AI clusters increasingly use single-mode even inside the building.

# 6. Attenuation: how little light is lost

Corning's SMF-28 Ultra loses at most 0.32 dB per km at 1310 nm and 0.18 dB at 1550 nm. 0.32 dB is about 7% of the light; a 2 km data-center link loses under 1 dB in the glass, less than its connectors. ATTENUATION comes from scattering in the glass itself and residual absorption.

# 7. Latency: the speed of light in glass

Light in glass travels at about two thirds of its speed in vacuum: roughly 5 microseconds per kilometre. Across a building that's negligible next to the electronics, but across a campus or between data centers it adds up. Hollow-core fiber, which guides light in air, cuts that by about a third.

# 8. Who makes it

Corning (GLW) is the largest maker of optical fiber, with Prysmian, Fujikura, Sumitomo Electric, Furukawa (OFS) and YOFC. Fiber is drawn from a glass preform in a tower, coated on the fly, and wound onto spools many kilometres long.

# 9. Chokepoints and risks

- AI data centers use many times more fiber per rack than cloud ones, straining cable and connector capacity.
- Tight bends leak light; dense cabling needs bend-insensitive fiber.
- Dirty connector end faces are a leading cause of link failures.

# 1. What happens: thin the wafer, cut it into die, pick the good ones

DICE is the back-end step that separates a finished wafer into individual die. First the wafer is thinned, then it is laminated onto blue dicing tape stretched across a steel ring frame. A saw blade or a laser cuts the wafer along the streets between die, and the tape holds the separated die in place. The picker takes only the die that passed wafer sort.

# 2. The parts

WAFER          a 300 mm silicon disc, about 775 um out of the fab, ground to 50-200 um
DICING TAPE    blue tape stretched on a ring frame, so the wafer can be cut and handled
STREET         the scribe lane between die, where test structures sit and the cut goes
KERF           the width of material the cut removes
BLADE          a thin diamond-edged saw blade, spun fast and cooled with water
STEALTH LASER  a laser focused inside the silicon, making a modified layer, not a groove
LOW-K          porous, brittle dielectric film in the top layers, prone to chipping
PICKER         the machine that lifts good die off the tape for die attach

# 3. Backgrinding first

The starting thickness is the first number to know: 750 um in, for a 300 mm wafer. Incoming wafers are 725-775 um thick (SemiconductorX), roughly 750 um per Wikipedia. The BACKGRIND step then removes most of that silicon from the back, down to 50-200 um: about 150-200 um for standard wire-bond and flip-chip die, 75-100 um for mobile, and below 50 um for stacked DRAM such as HBM. Thinning to 75 um removes about 90% of the silicon.

The wafer is held on a porous vacuum chuck while it is ground, so the thin silicon stays flat. The GRINDING WHEEL is a ring of diamond abrasive segments; only its rim touches the wafer. Coarse and fine passes leave a damaged layer that a final polish or etch removes.

At MOUNT, the front-side tape is removed and the wafer is laminated back side down onto dicing tape stretched across a ring frame. The frame makes the floppy wafer a rigid part that the saw, the laser, the expander and the picker can all grip.

# 4. Streets: how wide the lane is

Blade-cut streets commonly run 60-100 um wide, and about 80 um is a typical design. A narrower street leaves more room for die. On a 300 mm wafer, a 0.5 mm die gets about 24.5% more die when the street shrinks from 80 um to 20 um (computed with the standard gross-dies formula, before edge exclusion and yield). Larger die gain less, about 1-2% for 10 mm die.

# 5. Blade dicing

The DICE blade is a diamond blade that spins at 15,000-30,000 rpm for general dicing, and up to 50,000 rpm in advanced operations. Water cools the blade and flushes swarf. Resin-bonded blades cut kerfs of about 55-110 um, and metal-bonded blades about 20-25 um. The blade cuts through the wafer and into the tape, not through the tape, so the die stay held. Mechanical stress chips the top and bottom edges; a chip over 5-10 um may disqualify a die.

# 6. Stealth laser dicing

The STEALTH process focuses a laser at a wavelength silicon passes through to a point inside the wafer. At the focus, a modified layer of microcracks forms without touching the surface, and stretching the tape splits the wafer along the lines. There is no blade and no kerf. The SDBG variant, stealth dicing before grinding, lasers the wafer first while it is still thick and strong, then grinds it. The grinding removes the modified layer itself; DISCO says SDBG reduces front and back chipping and gives thin die higher strength.

# 7. Low-k: grooving before the cut

Low-k films are porous and brittle, and a blade alone can chip or peel them. Laser grooving followed by a blade is now standard for logic wafers. In GROOVING, a laser burns a pair of narrow grooves through the low-k and metal stack on each edge of the street, and then the blade cuts the silicon between them. Plasma dicing is the other route: it etches the street chemically. The cut edge matters because a chip that reaches the seal ring can let moisture in later.

# 8. Plasma dicing

Plasma dicing etches the streets through a mask instead of cutting them. A 2023 study on 10 x 10 mm dies compared blade, stealth and plasma dicing before grinding; the plasma-diced dies showed the least chipping and cracking and the highest strength. Equipment vendors claim larger gains. Blade dicing is still the most cost-effective choice in most cases, and plasma is most compelling for very small die or very thin wafers.

# 9. DAF, EXPAND + PICK, and yield

The back of the wafer carries DAF, the die attach film: a thin adhesive that becomes the glue holding each die to its substrate or to the die below. The film has to split with the die. DAF stretches rather than snaps at room temperature, so the expander runs cold, where the film is brittle and tears along the die edges.

In EXPAND + PICK, the tape is stretched radially. This widens the gaps so neighbouring die do not touch during pick-up, and it makes sure the stealth cracks or the DAF fully separate. Heat shrink then removes slack so die spacing holds on the frame. The picker uses the wafer map from wafer sort: only die marked good are lifted, and the rest stay on the tape. In a 12-high HBM stack every die must be known good, so a sorting miss multiplies.

Dicing is a yield step: each chip, crack or off-street cut can kill a die, and the loss is paid for only once the good die are packaged. Dicing yield on mature lines is reported above 99%, but that figure is from a vendor and is not a sourced industry average.

# 10. Who makes it, and the chokepoints

DISCO (6146.T) is the dominant supplier of dicing saws, grinders and blades, and it sells stealth and laser dicing systems. DISCO net sales were 436.889 billion yen for the fiscal year ended March 2026, per the company results PDF cited in the sources. Consumables such as blades and wheels are recurring and tied to wafer volume.

Risk: DISCO dominates saws, wheels and blades, and HBM stacks need wafers thinned to tens of um, so grinder and laser-saw supply gates AI memory output. This is an inference from the numbers, not a sourced shortage claim. Tokyo Seimitsu (Accretech, 7729.T) is the other large saw supplier; ASMPT (0522.HK), ADT and Okamoto also appear in the market. Laser dicing and grooving tools come from Hamamatsu (stealth engines), EO Technics, Synova and Oxford Instruments. Plasma dicing tools come from Plasma-Therm, SPTS (KLA) and Panasonic.

Lintec, Mitsui Chemicals, Nitto Denko and Furukawa Electric make the dicing tape, and Resonac, Nitto Denko, Lintec and Furukawa make the DAF. Each wafer uses tape and film once, and the film is qualified with a specific saw and expander recipe, so supplier switches take time.

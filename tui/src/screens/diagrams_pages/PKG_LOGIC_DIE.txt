# 1. What happens: the compute die, as big as one exposure allows

The logic die is the GPU or accelerator itself, built on a leading-edge node. A scanner prints one field at a time, and that field has a maximum size: the RETICLE LIMIT of 26 x 33 mm, 858 mm2. Big AI dies push right up against it (DIE SIZE): NVIDIA's H100 is about 814 mm2. Past that point the only way to get more silicon is to use several dies (CHIPLETS) joined by a fast DIE-TO-DIE link inside the package, and to place HBM stacks along the die's edges.

# 2. The parts

RETICLE LIMIT     26 x 33 mm = 858 mm2, set by the scanner's optics
DIE SIZE          H100 about 814 mm2, about 95% of the field (secondary sources)
YIELD             a defect kills the whole die it lands on, so large dies yield worse
CHIPLETS          several smaller dies in one package instead of one huge die
DIE-TO-DIE        the link between chiplets; Blackwell's two dies need about 10 TB/s
KNOWN GOOD DIE    each die is tested before packaging, since one bad die spoils the package
HEAT              the logic die makes most of the package's heat
HBM SHORELINE     HBM can only connect along the die's edges, so edge length limits memory

# 3. One die, in order

1. the design fills one reticle field
2. the wafer is exposed field by field and processed
3. wafer test marks the good dies
4. dies are cut out and only known-good ones go on
5. each is bonded beside its HBM stacks on an interposer

# 4. The reticle limit and die size

A scanner can't print a die larger than its field: 26 x 33 mm, 858 mm2. H100 at about 814 mm2 already uses about 95% of it. High-NA EUV halves the field in one direction (see the LITHO node), so single dies get smaller, not larger.

# 5. Yield: why big dies cost more

Defects land at random. On an 800 mm2 die each defect scraps 800 mm2; on a 200 mm2 chiplet it scraps 200 mm2. Estimates put yield near 45-47% for an 800-814 mm2 die at typical defect densities (secondary estimates). Smaller dies plus KNOWN GOOD DIE testing raise the yield of the whole package.

# 6. Chiplets and the die-to-die link

CHIPLETS split one design across dies joined in the package. NVIDIA's B100/B200 use two compute dies that need roughly 10 TB/s between them, which is why they moved to CoWoS-L with silicon bridges (Tom's Hardware). The DIE-TO-DIE link must be wide and short enough that software sees one GPU.

# 7. Edges for memory, and heat

HBM SHORELINE: each HBM stack connects along a die edge through the interposer, so more stacks need more edge, one reason packages are growing. Blackwell's memory bandwidth is about 8 TB/s (not to be confused with the 10 TB/s die-to-die). HEAT: the logic die is the main heat source, cooled from the top (see BOARD).

# 8. Who makes it

NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD design the dies; TSMC (TSM) builds them on N4/N3-class nodes and packages them with CoWoS. Intel (INTC) and Samsung also build large logic dies and their own packaging.

# 9. Chokepoints and risks

- The reticle limit caps single-die size; growth comes from more dies and bigger packages.
- Large-die yield losses raise cost per good die.
- Every extra die adds a die-to-die link to design and test.

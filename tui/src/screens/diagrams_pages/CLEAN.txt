# 1. What happens: strip organics, oxide, particles and metals, many times per chip

The clean step runs wet chemistry on the bare wafer.
Each chemistry removes one kind of contamination.
An ultrapure water rinse separates one chemistry from the next, so that acid and base never meet on the surface.
The sequence starts with the hot organic strip and works through the oxide, particles and metals, then rinses and dries.
Real fabs use many variants after almost every etch, strip and CMP step.

# 2. ORGANICS: the hot first step

Sulfuric acid plus hydrogen peroxide (SPM, also called piranha) oxidises photoresist and other carbon residue into CO2 and water, which is why it leads the sequence. The patent example runs 99% H2SO4 to 1% H2O2 at 140 C for 10 minutes, then an SC1 step at 55 C. The peroxide is consumed quickly at that temperature, so tools either dose fresh peroxide inline or recirculate and replenish a bath (textbook).

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/US7449127 ; https://patents.google.com/patent/US6153014

# 3. RINSE: DI water between every chemistry

The rinse is not a formality. Each chemical (acid, base, solvent) must be flushed before the next one touches the wafer, otherwise acid and base meet on the surface and form salts and particles. Ultrapure water plants are a chokepoint alongside the chemicals, and traces of dissolved ions, silica, bacteria or particles in the rinse can re-contaminate the surface just cleaned (textbook). The textbook water spec is 18.2 megohm-cm resistivity, with no source found.

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/US7449127 ; https://noeltech.com/cleans/

# 4. OXIDE: DHF strips native oxide

Dilute HF (usually 1000:1 or 200:1 HF to water) etches silicon dioxide, removing native or thin oxide and any contaminants trapped in it. The overall chemistry is SiO2 + 6HF giving H2SiF6 + 2H2O (textbook). After the dip the silicon surface is hydrogen-terminated, hydrophobic and very clean but also reactive, so it should go to the next process step or a controlled ambient quickly. Over-etching of thin gate oxides or low-k spacers changes device dimensions, so concentration, time and temperature are held tight, and HF is toxic.

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/US7449127 ; https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6843857

# 5. PARTICLES: SC1 undercuts particles

SC1 (ammonium hydroxide, hydrogen peroxide, water) removes particles by a mild etch-and-regrow cycle: peroxide grows a thin oxide, the base dissolves a little of it, and each cycle undercuts the particle (textbook). At a pH near 10 the particle and the wafer surface both carry a negative charge, so they repel and the particle stays in solution. The weakness is roughness and loss of material, so hotter and longer recipes trade particle removal against surface roughness and pitting. Advanced nodes pair SC1 with megasonic energy.

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/US7449127 ; https://siliconsemiconductor.net/article/117031/How_advanced_cleaning_helps_achieve_optimal_wafer_yields_at_advanced_semiconductor_nodes

# 6. METALS: SC2 turns metals into chlorides

SC2 is water, peroxide and hydrochloric acid. It converts metal contamination into soluble chlorides that rinse away. SC1 can leave behind or even deposit metals such as iron and aluminium hydroxides, which is one reason SC2 follows it (textbook). Transition metals and mobile ions diffuse into silicon and degrade gate oxide integrity and minority-carrier lifetime, raising leakage and failing devices without any visible defect. That makes metal control a yield matter, which is why chemical purity grades are specified so tightly.

Sources: https://noeltech.com/cleans/ ; https://aemd.sjtu.edu.cn/en/platform/882.html

# 7. SC1: recipe spread

SC1 is water, peroxide and ammonium hydroxide. The patent range on this page is 1000:1:1 to 1:1:1 by volume with a preferred 100:1:1 to 5:1:1, at 30-80 C for 5-30 min. A vendor bench uses 40:1:1 at about 50 C for 20 min. The gap shows there is no single standard: fabs tune ratio, temperature and time per layer. Too hot or too long gives roughness and thickness loss, and baths drift as ammonia and peroxide decay with temperature (textbook).

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/US7449127 ; https://patents.google.com/patent/US6153014 ; https://noeltech.com/cleans/

# 8. DRY: water must leave cleanly

After the final DI rinse the wafer must be dried without watermarks and without pulling fine features together. The simplest route is spin dry: spin the wafer to throw off water, often with N2 and an IPA vapour to lower surface tension. Where capillary forces would still collapse the structure, supercritical CO2 avoids a liquid-gas interface entirely. Patent text says its drawbacks are slow supply and discharge of the fluid and possible redeposition of IPA residue during depressurisation.

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/9437416 ; https://patents.justia.com/patent/9437416

# 9. chem nozzle arm: dispensing onto a spinning wafer

In a single-wafer tool the wafer sits face up on a spinning chuck or edge pins, and a swing arm brings the chemical nozzle across it. Spin speed creates the liquid film, and the spent liquid, with the particles it lifted, runs off the edge into the bowl (textbook). Each wafer gets fresh chemistry, which is the main advantage over a batch bench where one bath is reused by many wafers. The tradeoff is fewer wafers per hour, so a fab buys many chambers per tool.

Sources: https://envalith.com/en/companies/7735/segments ; https://marketsandmarkets.com/ResearchInsight/

# 10. megasonic transducer: sound that shears particles

A megasonic transducer sends acoustic waves at about a megahertz through the liquid film. The oscillating pressure creates microscopic bubbles, and their motion thins the boundary layer at the wafer surface and pushes loose particles that chemistry alone has undercut. Cavitation, or bubble collapse, is the double-edged part. A patent test predicts damage-free operation at 1 MHz or below, and a 0.1 ms pulse cycle gave no damage while a 2 ms cycle gave 1,216 damage sites on 55 nm features, so the usable window is narrow.

Sources: https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10910244 ; https://aemd.sjtu.edu.cn/en/platform/882.html

# 11. splash guard: bowl, rings and drains

The splash guard or bowl catches everything the spinning wafer flings off. In chemical tools it is often a stack of rings that rise and fall so each chemistry exits into its own channel. That keeps acid, base and solvent waste on separate drain lines, because mixing them would be dangerous and would ruin recycling (textbook). Cross-contamination shows up as watermarks, haze and particle adders on the next wafer when mist from one chemistry lands on another's path.

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/US7449127 ; https://envalith.com/en/companies/7735/segments

# 12. Marangoni: the meniscus pulls lines together

When water sits between tall, thin lines, the curved meniscus pulls them together as it recedes. If the lines are stiff enough they spring back, if not they stick, which is stiction. Marangoni and IPA drying introduce isopropanol so that surface tension is lower and varies along the receding edge, pulling water away from the surface (textbook). One Applied Materials abstract compares methods: solvent-assisted Marangoni drying gave about 40% stiction, sublimation drying about 90%, and supercritical CO2 drying none.

Sources: https://ecs.confex.com/ecs/228/webprogram/Paper55794.html ; https://patents.justia.com/patent/9437416

# 13. BATCH WET BENCH: batch versus single-wafer

A batch bench lowers a cassette of 25-50 wafers (the page's figure) into heated recirculating tanks of SPM, SC1, SC2 and DHF, with rinse and IPA dry tanks between. It gives high throughput per square metre but uses big chemical volumes, and all wafers share the bath, so cross-contamination is a risk. Single-wafer spin cleaning uses fresh chemistry on one wafer at a time, with tight control and lower damage on fragile patterns, but fewer wafers per hour. SCREEN SPE says it holds top global share in single-wafer, batch and spin scrubbers, with no number given.

Sources: https://envalith.com/en/companies/7735/segments ; https://marketsandmarkets.com/ResearchInsight/

# 14. Etch + clean: a slice of WFE

Yole data cited on this page puts etch and clean at about 27% of wafer fab equipment spending in early 2022 and about 19% in H1 2024, dry and wet combined. The drop is a mix effect, so the share is not a sign that cleaning is shrinking in absolute terms. The listed names on the page are SCREEN Holdings (7735.T), Tokyo Electron (8035.T) and Lam Research (LRCX), with SEMES as a Samsung affiliate (textbook).

Sources: https://yolegroup.com/strategy-insights/ ; https://reportprime.com/semiconductor-wet-etching-system-r3629/company

# 15. Report C 2023: why the market size is uncertain

The page shows three reports for wafer cleaning equipment: 7.39 (2024), 9.28 (2024) and 10.92 (2023) billion USD. The spread is a warning, not a data point. Aggregators differ in whether they count wet benches, single-wafer tools, scrubbers, dry clean, consumables or service (textbook inference). For investing, company segment results and SEMI equipment data are better anchors than aggregator headline figures.

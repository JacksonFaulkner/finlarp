# 1. What happens: the package meets the board, its power and its cooling

The finished package solders to a board through BGA BALLS, usually on a MODULE that carries the GPU, its power stages and its connectors. POWER DELIVERY brings hundreds of amps at under a volt from the board's VRM up through the substrate, bumps and interposer into the die. All of that power leaves as heat through a lid into a COLD PLATE, and at AI densities LIQUID COOLING replaces air. NVLINK and similar links tie GPUs together, and many modules fill a RACK.

# 2. The parts

BGA BALLS        solder balls joining the substrate to the board, at about 1 mm pitch
MODULE           the board carrying the GPU package, power stages and connectors
POWER DELIVERY   the path from regulator to transistor, losing voltage at every step
VRM              voltage regulators on the board, as close to the package as possible
COLD PLATE       a copper block with fins or channels pressed onto the lid
LIQUID COOLING   coolant pumped through cold plates instead of air
NVLINK           NVIDIA's GPU-to-GPU link (other vendors have their own)
RACK             many modules, power shelves and switches in one cabinet
TIM              thermal interface material: TIM1 between die and lid, TIM2 between lid and cold plate
LID              a nickel-plated copper cover glued to the substrate that spreads the die's heat
MLCC             small ceramic capacitors under the package that cover fast load steps

# 3. Power in, heat out, in order

1. the VRM steps board power down to under a volt
2. current climbs through BGA, substrate, C4, interposer, microbumps
3. the logic die turns it into compute and heat
4. heat crosses the lid into the cold plate
5. coolant carries it away to the facility

# 4. Power delivery

A Blackwell-class GPU draws roughly 1,000 W at a core voltage near 0.8 V, which is over a thousand amps. The VRM's phases switch 12 V and smooth it into a thick copper power plane; from there the current climbs a via, many BGA BALLS in parallel, plated through-holes in the substrate core, stacked microvias, C4 bumps, the interposer's TSVs and microbumps. Every level drops voltage by I x R: at 1,000 A a tenth of a milliohm costs 0.1 V, an eighth of the supply. MLCC capacitors on the board's back, right under the package, cover load steps faster than the regulator can react. VRMs sit as close as possible; vertical power (Power tab, VRM) puts them under the package, and backside power on the die (see TOP METAL) shortens the last step.

# 5. Cooling

The logic die is the main heat source. Heat crosses TIM1 (a metal or gel layer), spreads in the LID, crosses TIM2 (grease or a pad) and enters a copper COLD PLATE whose skived fins give the coolant a large area. Each interface adds thermal resistance, so the TIMs matter as much as the plate; at AI power densities, LIQUID COOLING through the plate replaces air, which also lets racks hold more GPUs. Future packages are expected to need thousands of watts (Tom's Hardware, TSMC roadmap coverage).

# 6. Modules and links

A MODULE carries the package with its power stages; NVLINK lanes run from the package through the board to switches so many GPUs act as one. Package size growth means larger modules and boards.

# 7. Racks

Many modules, power shelves, network switches and cooling manifolds fill a RACK (see the Power and Network+Cool tabs).

# 8. Who makes it

NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD design the GPUs and reference modules; TSMC builds the dies and CoWoS; SK hynix, Samsung and Micron (MU) supply HBM; Ibiden and Unimicron make large substrates. ODMs such as Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron and Supermicro (SMCI) build boards, trays and racks; Supermicro sells liquid-cooled GB200/GB300 NVL72 racks with its own CDUs and manifolds (SMCI 10-K, 2026). Flex (FLEX) sells cold plates and CDUs through JetCool, rated over 3,000 W (FLEX 10-K, 2025); AAON's BASX makes CDUs for racks over 100 kW (AAON 10-K, 2026). Regulators come from Infineon, MPS and Renesas, MLCCs from Murata and Samsung Electro-Mechanics, high-speed connectors and cables from Amphenol (APH); other maker names are textbook, not sourced here.

# 9. Chokepoints and risks

- Power per package keeps rising; delivery losses rise with it.
- Air cooling runs out; liquid cooling needs new infrastructure.
- Larger packages mean larger boards and more warpage at assembly; corner BGA joints take the most strain.
- Liquid cooling adds leak risk and needs CDUs, manifolds and facility water.

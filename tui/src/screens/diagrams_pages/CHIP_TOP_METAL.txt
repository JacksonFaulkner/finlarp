# 1. What happens: build the thick top metal, then the bump on top

The top levels use the same copper damascene loop as the thin ones, only wider and taller: TRENCH ETCH (dual damascene), BARRIER + SEED (TaN plus copper PVD), CU PLATE (copper fill), CMP (polish flat). Then the chip is finished for packaging: PAD + PASSIV (bond pads, SiN and polyimide with openings over the pads) and BUMP (under-bump metal and solder).

# 2. The whole stack, transistors to bumps

From the bottom: NMOS and PMOS in silicon; tungsten contacts; M1-M4 thin copper for cell wiring; M5-M9 medium copper for block signals; M10-M14 thick, wide copper for the power grid and clock trunk, with big vias between; aluminium or copper bond pads and RDL; passivation; C4 solder bumps at about 150 um pitch alternating power, ground and I/O; underfill; the organic package substrate. Wires get fatter going up: thin for density, thick for current and reach.

# 3. Jobs of the thick layers and the package side

POWER GRID: Vdd/Vss stripes feeding every cell. CLOCK TRUNK: the low-skew spine of the clock tree. LONG WIRES: buses crossing blocks. RDL: a redistribution layer re-routing pads to the bump pattern. UNDERFILL: epoxy under the die that spreads stress. Wire resistance is R = rho x L / (W x T), so thicker and wider wires cut it.

# 4. Power delivery and why droop happens

Current flows from the VRM on the board through package planes and capacitors, the bump array, M14 down through M10 and its vias, M5-M9, M1-M4, the cell rail and finally the transistor (0.9 V is an illustrative supply). IR DROP: every stage drops V = I x R, and the die sees what's left; designers tolerate roughly 10% total droop (secondary). EM LIMIT: copper can carry only so much current density before electromigration.

# 5. Connection density: bumps to hybrid bonds

C4 BUMP: about 150 um pitch, a solder ball from chip to substrate. MICROBUMP: about 40 um pitch (20-25 um bumps with 15 um spacing), solder-capped copper pillars from chiplet to interposer, expected to hit a wall near 10 um. HYBRID BOND: 10 um pitch and below, direct copper-to-copper and oxide-to-oxide with no solder, for die-to-die stacking. Smaller pitch means more connections per mm2.

# 6. Backside power delivery (BSPDN)

BSPDN moves power off the signal side. Today power and signals share the front stack; with backside power, the package bumps go on the back of the die, backside metals (BM0-BM5) carry power through the thinned silicon via nano-TSVs, and M0-M14 carry signals only. Intel's PowerVia (18A) uses nano-TSVs; TSMC's A16 Super Power Rail contacts the source/drain directly. PowerVia's test chip showed over 30% lower droop and over 6% speed; Intel's 2026 18A brief claims about 10x lower worst-case dynamic droop.

# 8. Who makes it

TSMC (TSM) is the foundry for NVIDIA, AMD and Arm-based chips, with A16 backside power late 2026. Intel (INTC) ships PowerVia backside power on 18A in Panther Lake (per teardown). NVIDIA and AMD build big GPUs and CPUs with huge supply current and microbump 2.5D/3D parts. Arm (ARM) core IP must fit the foundry's power grid. Synopsys and Cadence (SNPS, CDNS) sell IR-drop, EM and power-network sign-off tools.

# 9. How the top of the stack fails

- IR drop and droop from the M14 grid to the cell rail (I x R plus fast di/dt): wider grid, more bumps, capacitors.
- Electromigration in thin wires, vias and bumps: wider wires, redundant vias.
- Via and barrier voids in copper damascene: process control.
- Bump bridging at fine microbump pitch: copper pillars, hybrid bonds.
- Thermal-cycle fatigue of C4 joints from die/substrate expansion mismatch: underfill gives over 10x life.
- Notes: layer names M10-M14 follow the Chip tab; real stacks differ; top metal pitch and thickness are foundry-confidential.
